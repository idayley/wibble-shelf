# The method

The Discovery kit answers one question per market: **is anyone's hair on fire?**
It covers the first stage of *Nail It Then Scale It*, "nail the pain". You make
no product and no pitch. You talk to people about problems they already have,
score each call, and let a line you set in advance tell you whether to keep
going with that market or drop it.

Three agents help. **Scout** finds people, **Asker** drafts the messages and
call sheets, and **Debrief** scores each call. You send every message and make
every call yourself.

## 1. A bet per market

Write each market down as a bet that could turn out wrong: *who* has *what
pain*, and where you can find them. "Operations leads at mid-sized firms who
rebuild the same report by hand every week, found in their trade association's
forum" is a bet. "Small businesses" is not one, because you can't say where to
find them or what would prove you wrong.

Set the pass line **before the first call** (below). The board locks it once a
call is counted. Founders who state their tests up front are more willing to
drop a bad idea (Camuffo et al., 2020).

## 2. The ladder: one score per call, 0 to 5

The ladder is Steve Blank's list of earlyvangelist traits, used here as a score.
Blank describes a *type* of customer. Scoring each call on it is this kit's
adaptation.

| Level | Meaning | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | No evidence of the problem | "Not really an issue for us." |
| 1 | **Has the problem.** It shows up in a real past event. | "Last month we missed two deliveries because the counts were off." |
| 2 | **Knows they have it.** They call it a problem and it bothers them. | "That's the worst part of my week." |
| 3 | **Actively looking, with a timetable.** They have taken a step to fix it, or something recently broke their old way. | "I tried two tools last quarter." "We have to fix this before the audit." |
| 4 | **Built a workaround that still hurts.** A spreadsheet, a contractor, a manual routine that still costs them, and they aren't happy with it. | "It takes someone half a day a week and it still breaks." |
| 5 | **Has or can get budget**, and level 3 or higher is met. Money spent, set aside, or a concrete route to it. | "We paid for a tool last year and dropped it." |

**Hair on fire means level 4 or 5.**

Scoring rules:

- **No quote, no credit.** Each level needs something the person actually said
  about their own past or present.
- **Between two levels, score the lower one.**
- **A workaround they're content with is habit, not pain.** That's a 2 at most.
- **Compliments and hypotheticals count for nothing.** "Great idea", "I would
  use that", "we'd pay if it worked" are not evidence.
- **After a pitch, nothing scores above 2** unless it is about something they
  had already done.
- **If you can't tell who was speaking, never 4 or 5.** Recordings are
  transcribed without speaker labels, so Debrief works out who said what and
  marks its confidence low when it isn't sure.

A good call ends with a **commitment**: something the person agrees to give up,
with a what and a when. It can cost them time (a follow-up session with a
purpose), reputation (an intro to a named colleague or their boss) or money (a
deposit, a pre-order, a letter of intent). "Keep me posted" is not a
commitment. A commitment that hasn't happened 14 days after its due date lapses
and stops counting.

## 3. The pass line

The default is **3 of 10 calls at level 4 or higher, plus 1 commitment.**

Only some calls count toward it: people in the market's segment, not friends or
family, not calls where the speaker was unclear, and each person once. The board
works this out; the agents never compute a verdict.

The board then says one of three things:

- **Persevere** when the line is met, the hits come from at least two different
  organisations, and at least one hit holds the budget or makes the decision.
  This can happen before the tenth call.
- **Pivot** when ten counted calls are in and the line wasn't met, or earlier,
  once it can no longer be met. For example, no hits after eight calls against
  a line of 3 of 10.
- **Keep talking** otherwise.

You can override the verdict with a written note. The board shows your call next
to its own.

### Why these numbers

**Why 10 calls.** Ten is the floor Ash Maurya sets for problem interviews in
*Running Lean*. It is also at the low end of what research on interview studies
finds is enough to hear the main themes from a narrow group, which is 9 to 17
(Hennink & Kaiser, 2022). And a founder can do ten calls in about a week.

**Why 3 hits.** One hit is an anecdote, and two can be the same kind of person.

**Why a commitment too.** Three enthusiastic level-4 calls can all be polite
people who will never do anything. A commitment is the Mom Test's check that
the interest is real.

**What 3 of 10 can and can't tell you.** Ten calls is a small sample, so the
verdict carries a lot of chance. Two examples:

- If only **1 in 10** people in a market really have the problem this badly,
  the market still reaches 3 of 10 by luck about **7%** of the time. A false
  Persevere is rare.
- If **3 in 10** really do, the market misses the line about **38%** of the
  time, just from which ten people you happened to reach. A false Pivot is
  common.

So Pivot is the weaker verdict. At 10 calls it means "not clearly on fire
here", not "nobody needs this". Seen the other way round: if you get 3 hits
from 10 calls, the true share of people with the problem this badly could
reasonably be anywhere from about **11% to 60%**. Three of ten tells you there
is something there. It doesn't tell you how big it is.

**The close miss.** When a market ends at Pivot with one hit short of the line
and at least one commitment, the board offers to **extend to 15 calls**, with
the same number of hits needed. That trade has a price. At 15 calls, a true
3-in-10 market misses only about 13% of the time instead of 38%, but a true
1-in-10 market now passes by luck about 18% of the time instead of 7%. That's
why it is only offered on a close miss, and why you decide it in writing
**before** making the extra calls, not after seeing how they went.

(These percentages are binomial probabilities for 10 or 15 independent calls.
The 11–60% range is a 95% Wilson interval for 3 out of 10.)

**One more signal.** Watch how many people you ask actually agree to a call.
Furr and Ahlstrom treat that reply rate as evidence of pain in its own right. The
board's funnel (asked, then booked) shows it.

## 4. How a call should go

The short version, from the Mom Test and YC's "How to Talk to Users":

1. Talk about their life, not your idea.
2. Ask about specific things that already happened, not opinions about the
   future. "Tell me about the last time that happened" beats "Would you use…".
3. Start broad and see whether your problem comes up by itself before you ask
   about it.
4. Listen more than you talk.
5. Ask about what they've tried, what it cost, and who else was involved in
   deciding. At least one question should feel a little scary to ask.
6. End by asking for one concrete next step they could say no to.
7. Ask at the start whether you may record, and write down the answer.

Asker's call sheets are built on these rules. Debrief checks them after each
call and gives one thing to keep doing and one thing to change.

## 5. Honesty

- You say who you are: a founder of an early company, learning, not selling.
  Nothing after that turns into a pitch.
- A paid pilot or any other money ask comes up only as a follow-up to someone at
  level 5, never on a first call, and only if you have written the offer down.
- Warm introductions first, then email and LinkedIn messages. You send
  everything. No agent contacts anyone, scrapes a site, or keeps personal email
  addresses or phone numbers.
- Everything stays on your Mac: names, roles, links to public profiles, and the
  notes and scores from your calls.

## Sources

Each source did one job in the kit. "Checked" means the wording was checked
against the author's own site, an official page or the publisher's own material
while the kit was researched. "From the book" means it is cited by chapter and
was not re-read, so check your own copy before quoting it.

**Lean Startup: bets that can be proven wrong, and pivot or persevere**
- Eric Ries, *The Lean Startup* (2011): leap-of-faith assumptions (ch. 5),
  vanity metrics (ch. 7), the regular pivot-or-persevere meeting (ch. 8).
  From the book.
- Arnaldo Camuffo, Alessandro Cordova, Alfonso Gambardella and Chiara Spina,
  "A Scientific Approach to Entrepreneurial Decision Making: Evidence from a
  Randomized Control Trial", *Management Science* 66(2), 2020. In a trial with
  116 startups, founders trained to state hypotheses and test them were more
  likely to drop or pivot a project. Checked (abstract).

**Customer Development: the ladder**
- Steve Blank and Bob Dorf, *The Startup Owner's Manual* (2012), Figure 3.1
  "Earlyvangelist Characteristics": has a problem, is aware of it, has been
  actively looking for a solution, has assembled a solution out of parts,
  has or acquired a budget. Checked against the publisher's companion PDF,
  p. 13.
- Steve Blank, "Perfection By Subtraction – The Minimum Feature Set",
  steveblank.com, 4 March 2010. Adds that the active search comes "with a
  timetable", and that the problem is "painful enough that they have cobbled
  together an interim solution". Checked.
- Steve Blank, *The Four Steps to the Epiphany* (2005), the Customer Discovery
  chapters, where the list first appeared. From the book.

**Nail It Then Scale It: the scope**
- Nathan Furr and Paul Ahlstrom, *Nail It Then Scale It* (2011), Part I, "Nail
  the Customer Pain": find a pain customers recognise, will pay to fix, and want
  fixed now, before building anything. From the book.
- Paul Ahlstrom, "Discover the Monetizable Market Pain", Stanford eCorner
  (stvp.stanford.edu): a real pain is "a pain so significant that customers will
  return your cold calls". Checked.

**The Mom Test: how to ask, and how to score what you heard**
- Rob Fitzpatrick, *The Mom Test* (2013): the three rules (ch. 1); compliments,
  fluff and ideas as bad data (ch. 2); look before you zoom and the scary
  question (ch. 3); commitment and advancement, zombie leads (ch. 5); finding
  conversations and the five-part ask (ch. 6); narrow customer slices (ch. 7).
  From the book.
- Eric Migicovsky, "How to Talk to Users", Y Combinator Startup School (recap at
  ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky).
  The five past-behaviour questions, don't pitch, ask before recording, start
  with warm introductions. Checked.

**Jobs to be Done: the switch questions and the four forces**
- Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, "The Four Forces", jobstobedone.org: push of the
  situation, pull of the new way, anxiety of the new, habit of the present.
  People switch when push and pull outweigh anxiety and habit, which is why a
  workaround someone is content with counts as habit rather than pain. Checked.
- Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, "Unpacking the Progress Making Forces Diagram",
  jobstobedone.org: rebuild the timeline of a decision rather than asking for
  opinions. Checked.
- Clayton Christensen, Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David Duncan, *Competing
  Against Luck* (2016), and "Know Your Customers' Jobs to Be Done", *Harvard
  Business Review*, September 2016: workarounds ("compensating behaviours")
  as a place to find jobs. Background; not re-read.

**Hair on fire: the target**
- Michael Seibel, "The Real Product Market Fit", michaelseibel.com (also in the
  YC Library): find "problems so dire that users are willing to try half-baked,
  v1, imperfect solutions". Checked.

**Why a second reader scores every call**
- Abbie Griffin and John Hauser, "The Voice of the Customer", *Marketing
  Science* 12(1), 1993. One analyst reading a transcript found about 54% of the
  needs in it; several analysts together found nearly all of them. Debrief is a
  second reader beside you, not a replacement for your own notes. Checked.

**How many calls**
- Ash Maurya, *Running Lean* (2nd ed.), ch. 7 "The Problem Interview": at least
  10 interviews before judging. From the book.
- Monique Hennink and Bonnie Kaiser, "Sample sizes for saturation in
  qualitative research: A systematic review of empirical tests", *Social
  Science & Medicine* 292, 2022: 9 to 17 interviews for a narrow aim and a
  similar group of people. Checked (abstract).
- Greg Guest, Arwen Bunce and Laura Johnson, "How Many Interviews Are Enough?",
  *Field Methods* 18(1), 2006: most themes within 12 interviews. Checked
  (abstract).

All of these count interviews needed to *hear the themes*. The pass line asks a
different question, *what share* of a market has the problem badly, which is
why section 3 spells out how much chance a sample of ten carries.
